0. Framework: The Triadic Equation Driving the Sector and Technological Imposition (T11.5)

Debates on digital dentistry are usually trapped between two poles: on one side, the normative thesis ("what should be"; Kahneman, Sibony, Sunstein 2021; Wennberg 2002), which argues that clinical knowledge must move from tacit manual skill and personal memory (Polanyi 1966) into open digital files connected to a closed-loop learning system fed by patient outcomes; on the other side, the economic thesis ("what is"; Arthur 1989; Shapiro & Varian 1999; Akerlof 1970), which shows that market winners today are selected not by clinical survival curves, but by capital scale, consolidation, and vendor ecosystem lock-in.

While both axes are valid, this binary spine is incomplete. Real movement in the field is explained neither by an abstract quest for measurement nor by capital accumulation alone; the primary driving force operating above both is technological imposition (T11.5). In dentistry, a clinical need does not arise first and then go searching for a machine; on the contrary, hardware, software, and material manufacturers push new scanners, CAD modules, and multilayer blocks onto the market, and clinical "need" and indication boundaries are redefined after the device enters the clinic. Documented in innovation economics as technology push (Mowery & Rosenberg 1979) and in health economics as supplier-induced demand (Evans 1974; McGuire 2000; in dentistry Birch 1988, Grytten, Carlsen, Sørensen 1995, and Listl & Chalkley 2014), this mechanism is the true engine of digital dentistry.

Here the author adds a critical normative caveat to his own thesis: even when technological imposition expands the sector, it carries serious clinical and institutional risks of its own. Not every procedure that a device or software can execute is a procedure that should be performed given the patient's biological and financial reality. What should be is not a blind rejection of technology, but binding the measurement capacity that enters the clinic with the device to the patient's genuine benefit and non-coercive acceptance.

Counter-Thesis · Field Objection and Falsifier Test

Counter-thesis (S4, S5, S1, S10): The decisive structural failure in the market is not whether geometry is stored in a file, but that long-term patient outcomes (survival, remake, complication) remain invisible to patients under information asymmetry (Akerlof 1970). Moreover, because biological tissues behave stochastically, dentistry can never be reduced to a purely deterministic engineering loop; the master clinician's tacit judgment always draws the final boundary (Polanyi 1966).

  • Falsifier test: If centers operating structured outcome-recording and root-cause feedback loops fail to demonstrate a statistically significant advantage in 5-year restoration/implant survival and remake rates over comparable centers without such loops, the normative feedback-loop thesis is falsified.

1. Today's Global Standard: The Three-Axis "Conditional Default", Full-Arch Implant Workflow, and Provisional Restorations

1.1. Short-Span Digital Workflow: Not Absolute, but a Three-Axis "Conditional Default" (T1.1)

The literature frequently repeats that the intraoral scan → CAD → CAM workflow has become the unconditional global standard for single crowns and short-span fixed prostheses up to 3–4 units (Joda, Zarone, Ferrari 2017; Mangano et al. 2017; Mühlemann et al. 2018). In field reality, there is no single "global default"; whether digital workflow is the default splits conditionally across three axes:

  1. Country development level: Hardware and skilled-labor access produce vastly different realities across developed and developing nations, while in underdeveloped settings even basic clinical outcome measurement is absent.
  2. Patient financial capacity: For financially empowered patients, chairside single-visit digital restorations are indeed the default.
  3. Indication and tooth-loss burden: When the goal is aesthetic or minor restorative intervention (chairside CAD/CAM inlays, onlays, single crowns; Morimoto et al. 2016: high long-term survival in ceramic/resin inlays and onlays), the digital workflow is the default. However, in patients with severe tooth loss, a few-unit digital restoration cannot solve the case; an onlay patient and a patient with extensive edentulism do not belong on the same plane. In high tooth-loss populations, four- or six-implant supported fixed hybrid prostheses and conventional/complete dentures remain the global default treatments (Papaspyridakos et al. 2012).

1.2. Full-Arch and Multi-Implant Intraoral Scanning (T1.2)

Literature built on older scanner generations has long argued that full-arch implant scanning accumulates optical stitching error and cannot be used without verification jigs or photogrammetry. The author's field verdict is unequivocal: Next-generation intraoral scanners achieve full passive fit in full-arch cases; furthermore, in leading clinical practice, pre-planned surgical guides combined with AI-driven full segmentation in enterprise software allow direct immediate loading without even requiring a post-surgical scan—full-arch digital workflow is solved in the field and industry acceptance is now standard (field observation).

1.3. Mandatory Milled PMMA vs Brittle 3D Printing in Provisional Restorations (T1.3 / EK S0)

The sweeping generalization that "digital workflow equals 3D printing" breaks down under masticatory load. Alongside definitive restorations, long-term provisional restorations—especially implant-supported immediate and fixed provisionals—still require CAD/CAM milled PMMA (polymethyl methacrylate) blocks; 3D-printed provisional resins remain brittle under functional loading. This field verdict aligns directly with peer-reviewed evidence: in a meta-analysis pooling 76 in vitro studies (Valenti et al. 2024, J Prosthet Dent), prosthetic polymers fabricated by subtractive milling demonstrated statistically superior flexural strength compared to additive 3D printing (Hedges' g = -3.88; 95% CI: -7.20 to -0.58; p = 0.02), an advantage preserved after thermal aging (Hedges' g = -3.29; p = 0.04). Three-unit 3D-printed provisional bridges also exhibit significant fracture-load degradation after artificial aging and extreme sensitivity to build angle and post-curing protocols (Reymus et al. 2020, Clin Oral Investig; see also Revilla-León & Özcan 2019). Thus, while 3D printing is standard for working models and surgical guides, milled PMMA remains indispensable for load-bearing implant provisionals.

Counter-Thesis · Field Objection and Falsifier Test

Counter-thesis (S3, S4, S5):

  • Passive-fit and verification boundary in full-arch implants (S3, S4, S5): In long-span, multi-implant full-arch cases, cumulative optical stitching deviation makes passive-fit verification and scanning accuracy boundaries prior to a rigid one-piece definitive framework an active point of debate in the literature (classic in vitro baseline Imburgia et al. 2017, BMC Oral Health: 6-implant model trueness 60–106 µm; Ender, Attin, Mehl 2016, JPD: n=15, earlier scanner generations and precision measurement only; for standardized measurement methodology see ISO 20896-1:2019 [S3]). Prospective clinical trials comparing jigless full-arch workflows using next-generation scanners and AI segmentation are not yet published (NO SOURCE).
  • Time/cost axis in short spans (S4, S5): Even in short spans, digital workflows have not been proven to increase long-term restoration survival over well-executed conventional impressions; the proven gains are patient comfort (Gallardo et al. 2018), chair time, and an approximately 18% reduction in laboratory cost for single implant crowns (Joda & Brägger 2015).
  • Falsifier test: In a prospective clinical trial, if >4-implant full-arch monolithic prostheses fabricated solely from IOS and AI segmentation without verification jigs show screw-loosening, framework-fracture, and remake rates indistinguishable from the verified group, the verification requirement falls; if higher, the field observation is bounded.

2. The Türkiye Equation: The "Klinik Lab" Transformation, Expat ≠ Tourist Distinction, and Technology–Service Flow

2.1. Overcoming the "Clinic–External Lab Bottleneck" Thesis: The In-House "Klinik Lab" (T2.1 / T2.4)

Classical prosthodontic literature identifies incomplete work authorizations, occlusal records, and shade communication between the clinic and external dental laboratories as the primary source of remakes (Afsharzand et al. 2006; Lynch & Allen 2005). In pioneering centers in Türkiye, however, that diagnosis describes yesterday's model. Technological evolution has eliminated the requirement that clinic and laboratory occupy separate premises, giving birth to the "Klinik Lab" (integrated in-house digital laboratory).

Four concrete dynamics enabled this transformation:

  1. High-precision CNC milling units taking over the bulk of physical fabrication,
  2. Dentists directly mastering digital design and manufacturing workflows, reducing dependence on external technicians,
  3. Leading manufacturers integrating chairside hardware and software ecosystems specifically for in-clinic production (Fasbinder 2006; Wittneben et al. 2009; Morimoto et al. 2016),
  4. Rapid-sintering monolithic zirconia and rapid-crystallization lithium disilicate glass-ceramics.

Today, in leading centers, the vast majority of prosthetic restorations are completed inside the in-house Klinik Lab without ever leaving the building. While survey studies confirm rising scanner and 3D printer adoption in Türkiye (Topsakal et al. 2023; for dental school curricula see Kale & Özçelik 2018), the national prevalence of the "Klinik Lab" model and its national effect on remake rates remain unmeasured ((field observation)).

2.2. Health Tourism Time Pressure, "Expat ≠ Tourist", and the Türkiye–Europe Macro Equation (T2.2)

The marketing promise of "full-mouth permanent restorations in 3–5 days" compresses biological tissue healing and long-term follow-up phases, creating clinical and institutional risk. Yet cross-border patient flow is not homogeneous and requires a crucial socio-clinical distinction:

  • Expat (Gurbetçi) ≠ Tourist: Patients of Turkish origin living in Europe (expats/gurbetçiler) have flexible travel calendars, visit the same city and clinic multiple times a year, and do not suffer from osseointegration or follow-up discontinuity. By contrast, time pressure and follow-up disconnection concentrate heavily in foreign medical tourists locked into rigid return flights.
  • The Türkiye–Europe Macro Equation (Case Volume and Two-Way Transfer): The fundamental reason Türkiye developed an exceptionally advanced clinical dental execution capacity is that severe tooth loss and complex restorative case volume—reduced in Western Europe through decades of preventive care—remain very high in Türkiye. National epidemiological data confirm this disease burden: in Türkiye's national oral health survey (Gökalp et al. 2010, Community Dent Health, n=7,833), mean DMFT was 10.8 at ages 35–44 and 25.8 at ages 65–74, with complete edentulism reaching 48.0% in the 65–74 cohort; the WHO Oral Health Country Profile for Türkiye (WHO 2022, GBD 2019 data) likewise reports 38.6% untreated permanent caries in ages 5+, 18.9% severe periodontal disease in ages 15+, and 12.0% complete edentulism in adults 20+. This massive case volume accelerated the surgical and prosthetic learning curve of clinicians in Türkiye and created a global division of labor: Europe transfers dental technology to Türkiye, and Türkiye sells high-volume clinical service back to Europe.
  • The Prudent Institution Principle: Time pressure and quality disputes in commercial health tourism always carry reputational risk. Prudent institutions building long-term trust deliberately avoid "tourist-calendar" coercion; what they sell is not cheap speed, but personal attention, care, and high technology. (Regulatory note: Türkiye's earlier health tourism regulation of 13.07.2017/30123 was repealed by the Official Gazette of 26.04.2025, No. 32882, and promotional boundaries for international health tourism were re-codified under RG 12.11.2025/33075 Article 8.)
Counter-Thesis · Field Objection and Falsifier Test

Counter-thesis (S3, S4, S5):

  • Follow-Up Continuity and Complication Management in Cross-Border Care (S3, S4, S5): In cross-border care for tight-schedule tourist patients, the primary medical risk is compressing biological tissue healing and osseointegration timelines while disrupting long-term follow-up continuity and complication management: when biological or mechanical complications emerge months later after the patient has returned home, continuity of care and the chain of clinical accountability can break down (Turner 2008, Br Dent J, PMID:18500297). Peer-reviewed complication or return-rate data specific to Türkiye do not exist in the literature (NO SOURCE, unmeasured).
  • Falsifier test: If a prospective cohort separating tourist and expat subgroups demonstrates that 5-year complication and remake rates in cross-border full-arch patients match those of locally treated patients, the follow-up discontinuity thesis falls.

3. Restorative Material Selection Axis, Same-Day Chairside Veneers/Moulding, and Multilayer Zirconia

3.1. Material Selection Axis: Not "Anterior / Posterior", but "Purpose + Unit Count + Bar Support" (T3.1 / T3.2)

The textbook rule of thumb—"lithium disilicate for anterior teeth, zirconia for posterior teeth"—is scientifically and clinically invalid. Five-year survival of lithium disilicate single crowns is 96.6% (Sailer et al. 2015, Dent Mater, 67 studies), and cumulative 16.9-year survival across 2,392 posterior monolithic lithium disilicate restorations reaches 96.49% (Malament et al. 2021, JPD), with no survival difference between anterior and posterior single units (Benli et al. 2022; Turkyilmaz et al. 2023; Aswal et al. 2023 [S3]).

Under the author's field verdict, the real selection axis is structured in four tiers by treatment purpose, unit count, and substructure/bar support:

  1. Aesthetic and conservative single-unit restorations: When natural optical integration and adhesive bonding are primary, lithium disilicate glass-ceramic, composite (moulding / composite veneer), or feldspathic/hybrid ceramics are selected (Klein et al. 2025 [S3]; for implant single crowns see Lemos et al. 2024 [S3]).
  2. Multi-unit bridges and pontic spans: Where connector fracture resistance governs, monolithic or multilayer zirconia is selected (Pjetursson et al. 2026, IJP Part 1; Romandini et al. 2026, IJP Part 2).
  3. Full-arch fixed implant rehabilitations (4- or 6-implant supported fixed prostheses): To control passive fit, long-span rigidity, and porcelain chipping, CAD/CAM titanium-bar-supported zirconia is the standard (Caramês et al. 2019, COIR; Papaspyridakos et al. 2012, IJOMI).
  4. Mandatory / transitional / constrained cases: When a titanium bar cannot be used, fiber-bar (glass/carbon fiber-reinforced composite framework) support serves as the fallback (Menini et al. 2024, J Clin Med — bounded to mandatory cases given in vitro/short-term evidence).

3.2. Same-Day Chairside Veneers/Moulding and Full-Arch Multilayer Zirconia (T3.3 / T3.4)

The unreferenced claim raised by some external voices that "pressed ceramic or hand-layering still yields superior marginal fit over CAD/CAM milling in ultra-thin veneers" is an outdated rote assumption. Within the Klinik Lab workflow, chairside CAD/CAM milling completes both preparation/scanning and final veneer delivery on the exact same day; the same single-day execution applies to non-prep/minimally invasive composite veneers and moulding (Fasbinder 2006; Wittneben et al. 2009).

Conversely, in full-arch and multi-pontic restorations, zirconia is preferred, and next-generation multilayer / gradient zirconia blocks take over: combining a high-translucency aesthetic anterior segment with a high-strength functional posterior segment inside a single zirconia disc (Zhang & Lawn 2018, J Dent Res: balancing the yttria translucency–toughness trade-off via graded layering) is a direct outcome of technological imposition (T11.5) dissolving traditional material boundaries.

Counter-Thesis · Field Objection and Falsifier Test

Counter-thesis (S4, S5, S3): Because cubic-phase content in the incisal layer of multilayer (4Y/5Y-TZP) zirconia reduces fracture toughness up to half that of 3Y-TZP (Zhang & Lawn 2018), substituting zirconia for lithium disilicate in enamel-bonded single anterior units merely for inventory convenience compromises adhesive and optical performance (Klein et al. 2025).

  • Falsifier test: If a ≥5-year randomized clinical trial comparing multilayer zirconia and lithium disilicate in anterior single crowns and veneers shows equivalent debonding, fracture, and patient-reported aesthetic scores, the single-unit lithium disilicate distinction falls.

4. 3D Cephalometry, the "Natural vs Showy" Axis in Smile Design, and the Routine CBCT Debate

4.1. The Real Measure in Smile Design: Patient Acceptance, "Natural vs Showy", and the Norm Limit of Models (T4.1 / T4.2)

Academic literature emphasizes measuring 3D digital smile design via geometric overlay deviation (plan vs outcome) and blinded aesthetic ratings, while noting that in practice it often remains a 2D communication tool (Coachman & Calamita 2012; Saini et al. 2025 [S3]; Jain et al. 2024 [S3]).

The author's field verdict grounds aesthetic measurement in three clinical realities:

  1. Clinician–patient perception threshold gap: Even when the dentist or digital designer critiques micro-geometric details, the patient is delighted with the result. This matches classic studies showing that while orthodontists and prosthodontists detect sub-millimeter asymmetries, laypersons accept midline and gingival deviations up to 3–4 mm as natural (Kokich, Kiyak, Shapiro 1999, J Esthet Dent; Kokich, Kokich, Kiyak 2006, AJODO).
  2. The primary axis — "Natural Like My Own Teeth" vs "Showy": The decisive clinical split is not an abstract "Golden Ratio" (Ward 2007), but whether the patient wants teeth that look "natural like my own teeth" or "showy (bright white, hyper-symmetrical, prominent)". When an algorithmic model's morphological proposal is milled in the right material, it looks like a real tooth rather than synthetic plastic, and the vast majority of patients gladly accept it (Witt & Flores-Mir 2011, JADA; Parrini et al. 2016, AJODO). Patients who reject natural morphology in favor of showy aesthetics are the exception.
  3. The statistical "norm" limit of AI and design models: By their mathematical nature, current design models continuously converge on the statistical average (the normative natural mean); they have not yet made the leap required to generate bespoke individual character or intentional "showy" design (field observation; the author's model research in this domain is ongoing).

4.2. Routine CBCT and 3D Cephalometry: Field Default + Papilla Threshold vs Normative ALARA (T4.3)

Regarding cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) in aesthetic and prosthetic cases, what happens in the field and what radiology guidelines prescribe must be stated transparently as two distinct layers:

  • What Is in the Field (Author's Field Verdict): In a pioneering digital center, CBCT is the default for every patient (field observation). This follows directly from the principle in Section 9 that "if there is a device, there is measurement": CBCT is the foundational measuring instrument of the 3D virtual patient (Hendrickx et al. 2024 [S3]). In aesthetic cases, CBCT is used not only for implant bone volume, but to measure the interdental papilla threshold: when the vertical distance from the interproximal contact point to the crest of bone is ≤5 mm, the papilla is present in nearly 100% of cases, dropping to 56% at 6 mm and ≤27% at ≥7 mm (Tarnow, Magner, Fletcher 1992, J Periodontol, 30 patients / 288 sites). In the field, this contact-to-bone-crest threshold is queried automatically using 3D data from enterprise AI segmentation and agent-based workflow automation (field observation).
  • What Should Be (Normative Guideline Layer): Under radiation protection principles (ALARA / ALADAIP), every radiographic exposure must be justified by its capacity to alter treatment decisions and executed with the lowest possible dose protocol.
Counter-Thesis · Field Objection and Falsifier Test

Counter-thesis (S4, S5):

  • Indication boundary and ALARA principle (S4, S5): According to international radiology guidelines, CBCT is not a routine screening tool, but an imaging modality governed by case-specific clinical indication and dose optimization (Tyndall et al. / AAOMR 2012, OOOO; European Commission SEDENTEXCT 2012). In purely aesthetic or prosthetic cases without implants, routine CBCT exposure is controversial under current guidelines; moreover, there is no published clinical evidence that AI segmentation lowers exposure thresholds or reduces repeat scans (NO SOURCE).
  • Falsifier test: If aesthetic restorations planned via CBCT papilla/bone-crest measurement demonstrate superior 2-year papilla fill (absence of black triangles) and lower remake rates than cases planned with 2D radiographs and bone sounding alone, aesthetic CBCT indication gains clinical proof; if not, routine exposure remains strictly a field practice.

5. Artificial Intelligence and Agent Workflows: Automation Bias in Diagnosis vs Preventive Dentistry in Visual Preview

5.1. AI in Diagnosis: Narrow-Task Accuracy and the Automation Bias Boundary (Schwendicke 2020 & Mertens 2021) (T5.1 / T5.2)

AI models reduce time and inter-observer variance in narrow, repetitive tasks such as CBCT segmentation, cephalometric landmarking, and radiographic second reading (Schwendicke, Samek, Krois 2020, J Dent Res; for diagnostic trial methodology see Nagendran et al. 2020, BMJ and Liu et al. 2019, Lancet Digit Health).

However, regarding AI's impact on the clinician's treatment decision (invasive preparation/restoration vs conservative monitoring), critical clinical boundaries must be managed:

  1. Simultaneous increase in non-invasive and invasive decisions in enamel caries (Mertens et al. 2021, J Dent): In a cluster-randomized study of 22 dentists evaluating enamel lesions across 20 bitewings, AI support increased sensitivity while increasing both non-invasive and invasive treatment decisions together, prompting the authors to warn that high sensitivity can trigger invasive overtreatment via false positives.
  2. Gap between retrospective accuracy and clinical outcomes (Nagendran et al. 2020; Liu et al. 2019): The vast majority of image-based AI studies rely on retrospective datasets, and pixel- or lesion-level sensitivity alone does not guarantee superior patient or tooth survival.
  3. Automation bias and the clinician verification boundary (Parasuraman & Riley 1997; Goddard et al. 2012; Lyell & Coiera 2017): Suspicious findings flagged by high-sensitivity algorithms, when combined with automation bias, can create a risk of premature or unnecessary invasive intervention; therefore, diagnostic AI must remain a second reader and every positive finding must always be verified by the clinician (S4, S5).

5.2. Author's Field Verdict: Not Diagnosis, but "Preview = Less Tooth Cutting" (Preventive Dentistry) and Agent Automation (T5.2 / T5.3 / T5.4)

The clinical direction of AI must be pivoted from diagnosis to visual preview: In the field, preventive dentistry is paramount and cutting a natural tooth is the last resort (Edelhoff & Sorensen 2002, JPD: full-ceramic or metal-ceramic crown preparation irreversibly removes 63% to 72% of sound coronal tooth structure). That is why non-prep/minimally invasive moulding (composite injection/shaping) is preferred over cutting teeth for full crowns or veneers whenever possible.

AI is deployed to reinforce this conservative principle: showing the patient a realistic visual preview of the final outcome before touching the tooth or removing a single millimeter of enamel. While diagnostic AI flagging suspicious shadows can push clinicians toward more cutting, preview AI convinces both patient and clinician to cut less (choosing conservative moulding or minimal restoration). Agent workflows (including the papilla-threshold query in Section 4.2 and visual preview automation) accelerate this conservative decision loop. The true metric of success is not pixel accuracy, but the pre- vs post-preview tooth preparation rate versus conservative (moulding) treatment rate (T5.4).

Counter-Thesis · Field Objection and Falsifier Test

Counter-thesis (S5):

  • Administrative agents, data privacy, and prospective evidence boundary (S5): Administrative and lab-coordination agents are not zero-risk; scheduling errors, billing mistakes, or transmitting protected health data (Turkish KVKK Law No. 6698 and RG 21.06.2019/30808 Personal Health Data Regulation) to external servers without local verification creates direct legal and clinical liability. Moreover, no published randomized controlled trial has yet tested whether visual AI preview reduces clinical tooth preparation rates (NO SOURCE; a field hypothesis awaiting prospective clinical validation).
  • Falsifier test: In a prospective controlled clinical series, if aesthetic patients shown a pre-treatment AI visual preview do not choose conservative moulding over invasive crown/veneer preparation at a higher rate than the non-preview group, the "preview reduces cutting" thesis falls.

6. Private Dentistry Is a Distinct Service Sector: "Zero Waiting", Hospitality, and Regional Technology Transfer

6.1. Sector Definition: "There Should Be No Waiting" and Comfort as a Paid Service Contract Term (T6.1 / T6.2)

Many external voices judged waiting lounge design, coffee bars, or children's play areas through the lens of public hospital medicine, concluding that "there is no peer-reviewed evidence linking them to restoration survival, so they are secondary." The author's field verdict corrects this framing at the root: Dentistry is distinct from general hospital medicine, and private oral and dental health institutions constitute a distinct service sector within healthcare. A person entering a private clinic seeks a complete service experience commensurate with the fee paid within their financial means.

Within this framework:

  1. The primary process metric is not "short waiting", but zero waiting (Maister 1985).
  2. If a patient must wait, they should wait not in a sterile corridor, but at a coffee bar, with a safe play area for their child, surrounded by attentive hospitality—and in rooms where long procedures such as moulding are performed, the dental chair itself should be a massage chair.
  3. Scientific honesty requires stating clearly: there is no peer-reviewed trial proving that a coffee bar or play area directly increases implant or crown survival (NO SOURCE; for environmental effects on dental anxiety see Qi et al. 2021 [S3], Lehrner et al. 2005, Armfield & Heaton 2013, Ulrich 1984, Dijkstra et al. 2006). Yet in private dentistry, these elements are justified not like a pharmaceutical drug via RCTs, but as primary terms of a paid service contract (Bitner 1992; Berry, Wall, Carbone 2006 [S3]), the positive link between patient experience and clinical safety (Doyle, Lennox, Bell 2013, BMJ Open: 55-study review), and a legitimate quality signal under strict advertising bans (Spence 1973).

6.2. Top-Tier Hardware Fleet Selection: "Hospitality to the Service Buyer" and "Regional Technology / Specialist Transfer" (T6.3 / T6.4)

In the literature, choosing between a single leading hardware ecosystem and open-format (STL / PLY / DICOM) multi-vendor equipment is reduced to "vendor lock-in (Arthur 1989) vs single-service accountability." The author's field verdict reveals the real socio-economic mechanism behind top-tier hardware investment:

Assembling chairside CAD/CAM milling units, root-form titanium implant systems, surgical operating microscopes, high-ecosystem workstations across every operatory, and massage chairs for long restorative sessions serves two purposes:

  1. Hospitality (İkram) to the individual purchasing the service: It is material and technical respect shown in return for the fee paid by the patient.
  2. Regional Technology and Specialist Workforce Transfer: The fee paid by the patient does not merely purchase a single crown or implant; it finances the transfer of world-class technology and specialist clinical talent to a non-metropolitan city/region. (Note: No comparative RCT demonstrates a direct clinical survival difference between competing top-tier hardware ecosystems [NO SOURCE]; open file format portability must still be preserved.)
Counter-Thesis · Field Objection and Falsifier Test

Counter-thesis (S4, S5): Under information asymmetry, luxurious physical environments and high-end hardware signaling (Spence 1973) can occasionally act as a persuasive veil for overtreatment or inflated pricing because patients cannot directly audit clinical quality; meanwhile, closed vendor ecosystems lock the institution into long-term consumable and licensing costs (Arthur 1989). Therefore, actual waiting time (minutes), treatment-plan cancellation rate, and open-format export capability must be tracked as hard audit metrics.

  • Falsifier test: If removing physical hospitality zones in near-zero-wait clinics does not alter patient satisfaction or referral rates, the "primary service contract term" thesis weakens; if 3-year remake and data-error rates are identical between a closed single ecosystem and an open multi-vendor fleet, claims of closed-ecosystem technical superiority fall.

7. Institutional Memory and Know-How: Not a Solved Recipe, but a "Two-Way Paradox / Dead End" (T7.1 / T7.2)

On the question of how clinical know-how remains in the institution rather than walking out with a single clinician, most external voices answered that "it is retained via standard operating procedures (SOPs), checklists, and locked CAD/CAM parameters" (Nonaka 1994; Haynes et al. 2009; Reason 2000). Yet strong counter-evidence shows that clinical skill lives in the triad of "person + team + routine": annual restoration failure rates vary 2.7-fold (2.6% to 7.0%) across individual dentists (Laske et al. 2016, JDR Clin Trans Res), and mandatory surgical checklists alone do not automatically reduce mortality or complications (Urbach et al. 2014, NEJM; see also Huckman & Pisano 2006, Mgmt Sci on hospital-specific team familiarity and Edmondson, Bohmer, Pisano 2001).

Rather than picking one pole, the author's field verdict honestly diagnoses the real "dead end" confronting the sector:

  • Every institutionalization step taken today (digital workflows, quality standards, the in-house Klinik Lab) aims to reduce dependence on individuals, because without an institutional identity, no center can survive long-term.
  • Yet there is a two-way paradox: An institutional identity cannot give a patient warm human care like a person does, and artificial intelligence cannot fill that human void. At the same time, neither a founding clinician's personal warmth nor a master surgeon's manual dexterity is permanent.
  • Digital workflows preserve the technical floor by moving prep margins, design parameters, and deviation metrics into files (T7.3); however, this dead end between institutionalization and personal warmth/skill remains unresolved (the author's open question).
Counter-Thesis · Field Objection and Falsifier Test

Counter-thesis / Complementary confirmation (S4, S5): No peer-reviewed study in dentistry directly compares the long-term patient outcomes of corporate/institutional structures against personal doctor–patient continuity (NO SOURCE); therefore, the issue must not be papered over with management slogans and must remain an open research question.

  • Falsifier test: In centers where founding/senior clinicians depart or hand over cases, if institutions running digital parameter libraries and structured master–apprentice pairing maintain 3-year complication, remake, and patient-retention rates unchanged from pre-departure levels, the "dead end" is partially overcome.

8. Quality and Accreditation (SKS): "Zero Impact on the Patient" and True Outcome Metrics (T8.1 / T8.2 / T8.3)

Türkiye's Health Quality Standards (SKS-ADSH Set Version 3, 2024 [S3]) and hospital accreditation programs audit structure and process paperwork out of Donabedian's (1988) three dimensions, not clinical outcomes. In organizational sociology, this is documented as the audit ritual (Power 1997), institutional isomorphism (Meyer & Rowan 1977), and hitting the target while missing the point (Bevan & Hood 2006).

Even the softened draft sentence—"SKS does not guarantee clinical outcomes, but is assumed to raise baseline safety"—is invalid before the author's field verdict and primary literature:

  • Author's Field Verdict: Bureaucratic quality certificates such as SKS have zero impact on the patient. The patient neither knows nor sees these folders; the process merely generates compliance data for inspectors and imposes a heavy administrative workload on the clinic. Even if narrated on a clinic's website, it carries zero meaning or effect for the patient.
  • Primary Literature: In a study of 4.2 million patients across 4,400 US hospitals (Lam et al. 2018, BMJ), hospital accreditation was not associated with lower 30-day mortality; Cochrane (Flodgren et al. 2011) and systematic reviews (Brubakk et al. 2015; Greenfield & Braithwaite 2008; process-focused reviews Hussein et al. 2021 and Alhawajreh et al. 2023 [S3]) likewise confirm that accreditation has no proven effect on patient outcomes. In dentistry, not a single study measures the relationship between SKS scores and clinical survival (NO SOURCE).
  • What Should Be: True quality measurement is not bureaucratic paperwork, but indicators tied directly to patient outcomes: waiting time, remake rate, biological/mechanical complication rate, and patient-reported oral health quality of life (OHIP-14; Slade 1997).
Counter-Thesis · Field Objection and Falsifier Test

Counter-thesis (S1, S2, S3): Even when invisible to patients, standardized sets like SKS may act as a mechanical floor limiting catastrophic baseline failures in sterilization traceability, radiation shielding, and surgical time-out verification (Hussein et al. 2021).

  • Falsifier test: If a significant dose–response difference in infection, complication, and retreatment rates is measured between high- and low-scoring SKS dental centers in Türkiye, the "zero impact on the patient" verdict falls.

9. Approaching the Global Standard: "If There Is a Device, There Is Measurement" and the "No Imposition" Principle

9.1. Rejecting "Measurement First, Device Second": "If There Is a Device, There Is Also Measurement" (T9.1 / T9.4)

Ten external voices converged on the textbook recommendation that "a clinic wishing to approach global standards must first establish paper outcome registries and protocols, and buy hardware later" (Porter 2010; Porter & Lee 2013). The author's field verdict rejects this artificial sequence:

  1. If there is a device in the clinic, there is already measurement. An intraoral scanner, CBCT, and CAD software are themselves measuring instruments; plan-versus-outcome deviation cannot be measured without a scan file in the first place (David 1990; Brynjolfsson, Rock, Syverson 2021). Measurement cannot be built as a paper bureaucracy prior to and independent of the measuring instrument.
  2. Psychological acceptance mechanism: People resist other people (verbal persuasion or manual rules), but when a high-technology device and screen are present, both the sector/clinician and the patient exhibit unconscious acceptance. This phenomenon aligns directly with the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM / UTAUT) (Davis 1989; Venkatesh et al. 2003; in healthcare Holden & Karsh 2010) and automation bias literature (Parasuraman & Riley 1997; Goddard et al. 2012; Lyell & Coiera 2017).
  • What Is vs What Should Be: What is: Centers buy the device, the device generates thousands of digital measurements every day, yet the data go unread and unlinked to a feedback loop. What should be: Not demanding paper protocols before hardware, but reading, verifying, and binding the measurement that already enters the clinic with the device into the clinical decision loop.

9.2. The "What Not to Do" List and the "No Imposition" Principle (T9.2 / T9.3)

A center operating at global standards observes four non-negotiable red lines:

  1. Zero before/after visual marketing, zero patient testimonial posts, and zero superiority claims: Under Türkiye's Regulation on Promotional and Informational Activities in Healthcare Services published in the Official Gazette of 12.11.2025, No. 33075 (repealing Regulation 29.07.2023/32263 via Article 13/1), Articles 5/1-e,h and 7/1-ğ,j,k strictly prohibit covert/overt advertising, device/brand superiority claims, patient thank-you/satisfaction visuals, sponsored visual boosting, and visual posts left open to comments, likes, or reposts.
  2. Zero unindicated cutting of sound teeth and zero "3-day permanent full-mouth" cutting: Biological healing and osseointegration timelines cannot be bypassed for marketing promises (Edelhoff & Sorensen 2002; Turner 2008).
  3. Zero external transfer of patient data without local verification and legal basis; zero unverified AI directly driving clinical decisions (KVKK Law No. 6698; RG 21.06.2019/30808).
  4. Zero time imposition or technological imposition on the patient (Author's Core Principle): In health or aesthetics, coercion/imposition is unacceptable from any angle. Forcing a patient into a compressed tourism calendar is unacceptable, and imposing a procedure solely because a new device or material sits in the clinic carries serious risk as well.
Counter-Thesis · Field Objection and Falsifier Test

Counter-thesis (S4, S5): A raw optical or radiographic file generated by a device is only a geometric record; unless remake and complication outcomes are linked back to that file, "owning the device" alone does not equal clinical quality measurement, and every field observation must be tested against its pre-defined FALSIFIER (DÜŞER) measurement (S4, S5).

  • Falsifier test: If 3-year remake and complication rates do not differ between centers that bind hardware data to outcome review loops and centers that merely purchase hardware, the requirement to bind device measurements to a feedback loop falls.

10. The Four Most Repeated False Claims ("Unconditional Generalization") and the "Decision Privacy" Principle

The four most frequently repeated false claims in clinical marketing and superficial literature summaries are:

  1. "Digital impression is unconditionally more accurate than analog impression in every case." (While highly accurate in short spans and with next-generation scanners, accuracy depends on arch span, implant count, scanning strategy, and operator experience: Imburgia et al. 2017; Ender, Attin, Mehl 2016; ISO 20896-1:2019.)
  2. "Artificial intelligence diagnoses better than the dentist and will soon replace the clinician." (Most comparisons are retrospective [Nagendran et al. 2020; Liu et al. 2019], high sensitivity can increase both non-invasive and invasive decisions together via false positives [Mertens et al. 2021], and automation-bias risk makes clinician verification mandatory [Parasuraman & Riley 1997; Lyell & Coiera 2017].)
  3. "Zirconia is the single superior and most durable material for every indication." (As translucency increases, fracture toughness drops [Zhang & Lawn 2018]; in adhesively bonded single units, lithium disilicate and conservative composite/moulding excel in their respective indications [Sailer et al. 2015; Malament et al. 2021; Klein et al. 2025].)
  4. "Extracting a questionable tooth and placing an implant is more durable and predictable than trying to save the tooth." (Long-term clinical reviews prove that natural teeth preserved through proper endodontic and periodontal care match or exceed implant survival while avoiding high long-term peri-implantitis risk: Tomasi, Wennström, Berglundh 2008, J Oral Rehabil; Levin & Halperin-Sternfeld 2013, JADA; Derks & Tomasi 2015, J Clin Periodontol.)

The Shared Methodological Flaw ("Unconditional Generalization") and the Author's New Thesis: "Decision Privacy" (Madde 19)

The common methodological error across all four claims is unconditional generalization ("always", "everywhere", "in every patient"). Here the author's field verdict establishes a permanent clinical-ethical principle:

  • Every case carries its own conditions, and the patient's financial capacity is directly one of those clinical case conditions.
  • The Principle of Decision Privacy: The treatment decision is private between the patient and the clinician. Not performing the textbook "gold standard" or the most expensive digital protocol in a specific case may be conditionally the right decision given the patient's budget, time, systemic health, anxiety level, or natural-versus-showy expectation—and that decision strictly does not concern third parties (social media spectators, external audit narratives, or colleagues unaware of the case context). Defined in medical literature as shared decision making (SDM) (Elwyn et al. 2012, J Gen Intern Med) and patient autonomy, true quality is measured not by conformance to an external template, but by the outcome and satisfaction achieved within the patient's own conditions.
Counter-Thesis · Field Objection and Falsifier Test

Counter-thesis (S4, S5): When "myth-busting," generalizing 2013–2016 data from early-generation scanners or materials onto today's hardware creates a reverse myth of its own (S4). Furthermore, "decision privacy" can never be used as an excuse for incomplete informed consent or for ignoring biological boundaries (such as active infection or non-passive framework fit); the clinician is always obligated to verify positive findings and biological limits (S5).

  • Falsifier test: If a 10+ year comparison of questionable teeth with similar baseline prognosis shows superior patient-reported function and cost-effectiveness for extraction + implant over natural tooth preservation, the fourth claim ceases to be a myth.
