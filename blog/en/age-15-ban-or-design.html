Ban or design?

Atlas Stüdyo analizi: Five things that would make the age-15 rule work

The regulation setting an age limit for social media was published in the Official Gazette on 10 October 2026 and takes effect on 1 November.[1] The debate has largely squeezed into one question: is a ban for under-15s right or wrong? This piece shifts the question a little.

It looks less at the ban itself than at the rules written beside it and how they will be measured. We also had the strongest objection to the view most models agreed on written by a separate counter-voice; that objection is in this piece too.

What was introduced

The regulation has two layers. The first is the age limit: children under 15 cannot open a social media account. The second is rules for those above the limit: accounts of 15–18-year-olds will run with the default settings listed in Article 8 of the regulation.

How age is proven is also described. The user presents the platform with an "age token," generated through e-Devlet (the national e-government portal) and carrying only the age threshold. Technical details of the token will be set by a guide the Presidency will publish (art. 12). That guide has not yet been published.

The text also contains parental tools (art. 9) and an obligation to publish annual statistics (art. 10).

Three approaches worldwide

The paths other countries have tried fall roughly into three models. To understand Türkiye's regulation, first look at what they produced.

Model 1

The ban model

An account ban below a certain age.

Australia (under 16, December 2025)[5], France's 2023 law (not enforceable)[6], US state laws (patchy, in court)[7][8].

Model 2

The design-rules model

Rules that say how a product must behave by age.

United Kingdom (Online Safety Act and Age-Appropriate Design Code; announced an under-16 ban in June 2026)[9][18], EU (Digital Services Act, age-verification app pilot)[10][11].

Model 3

The time-limit model

Hour or duration bans.

South Korea (2011–2021, repealed)[12][13], China (2021 gaming-time limit, minor mode)[14][15].

The line is fading: from design to ban

In the past year the two models have drawn closer. In November 2025 the European Parliament, in a non-legislative resolution, called together for an EU-wide minimum age of 16 (13–16 with parental consent) and a ban on the most harmful addictive design practices.[19] The country that has tried design rules longest is the United Kingdom.

According to Ofcom research released in March 2026, 72% of 8–12-year-olds there accessed sites and apps with a 13+ limit.[20] In June 2026 the government announced a ban on the service for under-16s; the rules are expected to take effect in spring 2027.[18] Türkiye building both models together fits this trend.

How well does a ban hold: Australia's first three months

The most recent measured example of the ban model is Australia. The country's online safety regulator eSafety published findings on the ban's first three months in July 2026.[2][3] The picture: the ban reduces the number of accounts, but use only a little.

Account ownership fell markedly; use fell only about 4–5 points.[2][4] The reason is less children's cunning than platforms' enforcement: of children who still have an account, 50.2% say the platform never asked their age and 18% say their age was wrongly estimated.[3][4] Three months may also be too short to show the ban's main effect: age limits leave their mark mostly on the cohort that has no account yet. That objection is in Thesis 2.

Time limits: Korea's lesson

South Korea introduced a night-time gaming ban in 2011. It was found ineffective[12] and repealed in 2021.[13] The shared view of the models in the round is that hour and time bans are the weakest of the three. The counter-voice objected with Norway's school phone ban; both views are in Thesis 5.

An age limit decides who gets in; design rules decide what happens inside. If neither is measured, we cannot know which one works.

Theses

The round's main output is six theses. Each card has the thesis's reasoning, the strongest opposing view and the measure that would change our mind. Below each card is every model's stance on that thesis: defending, partly agreeing, opposing and not addressed. Stances were measured by quotation from each model's own answer.

Thesis 1

An age limit alone produces no result; what protects is the design rules written for ages 15–18.

Why

Concrete paths of harm (messages from strangers, the child appearing in recommendations, uninterrupted feeds, phone-book matching) are closed by default settings, not by age. A default setting is cheap for the platform, hard for the child to get around, and applies to everyone at once. Article 8 of the regulation spells them out: private accounts, messages only from followed accounts, a ban on infinite feeds and personalisation.

Opposing view

A design rule can't be applied without knowing the user's age; so Article 8 doesn't work without an age limit and age verification. And the number of changes platforms made doesn't by itself show children are less harmed. None of the independent answers openly opposed the thesis; three partly agreed.

Gemini 3.1 Pro praises Article 8 as "a direct scalpel thrust into platform architecture," yet also writes that the UK and EU risk-based path "doesn't work." For the Gemini app the decisive factor is not the design rule but state-backed verification. Studio agent 2 sees the weak link not in the ban but in the verification method.

Counter-voice

Those who tried design rules longest found them insufficient and turned to an age limit. While design rules were in force in the UK, 72% of 8–12-year-olds accessed sites and apps with a 13+ limit[20] and in 2026 the government announced an under-16 ban.[18]

Of the safety tools Meta lists for teen accounts, 47 were tested: 64% either no longer existed or were ineffective, and only 17% worked as described. The report is by child-safety advocacy organisations.[21]

What would change our mind

If, 6 months after entry into force, independent test accounts show the large platforms largely implement Article 8 but complaints of unwanted contact from strangers don't fall, the "design protects" thesis weakens.

Thesis 2

An account ban reduces accounts, and use less.

Why

The ban's legal object is the account, while what consumes a child's attention is the feed. In most places the feed can be watched without an account; messaging and games fall outside the scope. So the easiest thing to measure (the number of accounts) falls, and the real target (how much, and to what, one is exposed) moves less.

Opposing view

Three months is too early; a 10-point fall in account ownership is not small. Australia had no state token; Türkiye's model tries to close exactly the verification link where Australia stayed weak. All eight independent answers agreed with this thesis; none opposed.

Counter-voice

The wrong measure was chosen. Age limits affect those already started little and those not yet started a lot; the right measure is not the account count after three months but the age at which the first account is opened. Even the 21 drinking age, which everyone knows is circumvented, clearly reduced consumption and the main harms in the US.[22] In Australia too, perception moved before behaviour; the numbers are in The counter-voice.[23]

What would change our mind

If, after 31 March 2027, an independent survey shows use at ages 13–14 fell by more than 20 points against before entry into force, this thesis falls for Türkiye.

Thesis 3

Privacy is decided not by the token's content but by how it is produced and verified.

Why

That the token carries only "has turned 15, has turned 18" is a right start. But three questions are open: is the token single-use, can tokens on two platforms be linked to the same person, does the platform ask the state each time to verify? Depending on the answers, the system is either a good example or a central register showing who enters which platform.

Having platforms collect ID photos is worse: about 70,000 users' ID photos leaked from the contractor reviewing Discord's age appeals.

Opposing view

The aim is not a flawless system but to stop a 13-year-old opening an account effortlessly. The state already knows more than this from other records; the token adds very little. Of the independent answers only Gemini 3.1 Pro partly diverged: its proposal wants a unique token per platform and session, yet it also presents state guarantee as what "takes the power to weaponise data out of platforms' hands."

Counter-voice

The comparison uses the wrong baseline. Today the party that already knows the child's age, interests and friends is the platform; in 2024 the US Federal Trade Commission said large platforms keep users under "vast surveillance" and don't adequately protect children and teens.[24]

Against this baseline the token reduces data: the platform sees not identity but only the threshold. Waiting for a flawless token can mean, as in France, enforcing nothing at all.[6]

What would change our mind

If the Presidency's technical guide defines the token as single-use, unlinkable across platforms and verifiable without querying the state, this concern falls.

Thesis 4

The main leak is not VPNs; it is token sharing at home and the shift to apps outside the scope.

Why

A token that carries no identity can by nature pass from hand to hand; for a child whose sister or mother generates the token, the ban effectively doesn't exist. A child leaving a banned platform can also move to messaging and in-game chat. If the measure is "account on a banned platform," success looks visible but harm relocates.

Opposing view

Privacy and unshareability can't both be fully achieved at once; the regulation chose privacy, and that is the right choice. No independent answer opposed; three partly agreed. Claude Opus 5.5, Gemini 3.8 Flash and Grok count token handover and drift outside scope as breakpoints, but also count VPNs as a leak route of equal weight; they rank no "main leak."

Counter-voice

The leak is not the measure of the ban's success: a leak is laborious and lowers the network's value. On migration, the data may say the opposite of this card: in Australia daily messaging use rose from 40.5% to 52.3%.[4] A move from algorithmic, stranger-open feeds to talking with acquaintances may not be harm relocating but an exit from the harm paths listed in Thesis 1.

Nor has VPN's insignificance been measured: after age verification began in the UK, one VPN company reported new sign-ups from the country up by more than 1,400%. That is the company's own statement, in the context of access to adult sites.[9]

What would change our mind

If in the first-year survey the answers to "whose token did you enter with" and "which app did you move to" stay at small rates, this thesis weakens.

Thesis 5

Hour and time bans are the weakest path.

Why

A time ban cuts one activity on one channel; the child carries the same hour to another app, device or an adult's ID.

Opposing view

South Korea's ban covered only online games on PCs, not mobile; a badly designed ban's failure says little about a well-designed one. Türkiye's regulation also sets no time ban, only gives parents a time tool. No independent answer opposed; three voices didn't address the topic.

Counter-voice

One example can't judge a whole class. An example where a time and place restriction can work is the school phone ban. Bans in Norwegian middle schools had no average effect on education or mental health; but bullying fell for both sexes, girls' health-service contacts for mental health fell, and girls' grades rose.[25]

The common point: the restriction applies to everyone at once, and at that hour there is no other channel to escape to. The UK plan also includes a default access restriction for ages 16–17 from midnight to 6 a.m.; the teen will be able to change this setting.[18]

What would change our mind

If an independent, peer-reviewed study shows a time restriction produced a lasting fall of 30 minutes or more a day in total screen time, this thesis falls. We found no such study.

Thesis 6

A 15–17-year-old who can't get a token is banned though not the ban's target.

Why

The system ties "hasn't turned 15" and "couldn't prove they have" to the same outcome: no account. Those who can't prove it aren't random: those without e-Devlet access, those whose password is with a parent, or foreign youths living in Türkiye.

Opposing view

An e-Devlet password can also be obtained with an ID card and mobile banking; access above 15 may be high. No independent answer opposed; five voices took no stance on this.

Counter-voice

The share who can't prove it is unknown; the thesis is built on an unknown number. Sensitivity also doesn't end at 15: the ages at which more social media use predicts lower life satisfaction a year later are 11–13 in girls and 14–15 in boys; in both, also 19. The study is observational and doesn't show cause and effect.[26] The solution is not loosening the limit but opening a second path; that is the same place as our fourth recommendation.

What would change our mind

If the first annual report shows the share of 15–17-year-olds able to generate a token at 95% or above, and a non-e-Devlet path is defined for the rest, this thesis falls.

What Türkiye's model does well

Türkiye did not pick one of these three paths. It is building an age limit, design rules and a central age token produced by the state all at once; a rare combination worldwide.

The least-discussed part publicly is Article 8; why these rules do more work than the age limit is in Thesis 1. For accounts of ages 15–18 the regulation says:

  • No swipe or auto-advancing visual-content interface may be offered. This means a ban on infinite feeds and autoplay.
  • The account can't be personalised by the child's behaviour; profiling and personalised ads are banned.
  • Live streaming can't be opened.
  • Screenshots can't be taken.

These articles make Türkiye's 15–18 rules among the strictest in the world. A note: in the round's first summary we too had skipped these four rules, and some models suggested them as "things to do." Yet they are already in the text. What is missing is not the rule itself but being able to see from outside whether the rule is obeyed.

The infrastructure is also ready. So Türkiye can do in three weeks what France couldn't in three years. For the same reason, the risk is also large.

Where it breaks

The models didn't openly oppose any thesis. Where they diverged is partial, but those are the places that decide. The reasoning and measure for the breakpoints are in the thesis cards; here are only where the models diverged and where we ended up.

The token: privacy is decided by operation, not content

Open questions and the measure are in Thesis 3. The divergence here is small but instructive. Studio agent 3 said infrastructure produced by a single institution is the structure most prone to expand over time to thresholds beyond age.

Gemini 3.8 Flash too wrote of the risk of an access map forming on the state side, showing who enters which platform. Gemini 3.1 Pro, by contrast, found the state guarantee positive in that less data goes to platforms.

Where we ended upFix the token's form at two bits (15+, 18+) and publish the specification openly.

Handover and migration: the token changes hands, harm relocates

Five voices (the studio's three agents, Gemini 3.1 Pro and the Gemini app) saw the main leak at home and in out-of-scope apps. Three voices (Claude Opus 5.5, Gemini 3.8 Flash and Grok) counted VPNs with equal weight (Thesis 4).

The real divergence is in the remedy. Gemini 3.1 Pro wanted more identity: NFC ID reading, live face verification. Studio agent 3 argued the opposite: a token without identity passes from hand to hand by nature. The regulation chose privacy, and chose rightly, but it must measure the cost.

Where we ended upThe second view. Handover is managed by measurement, not by prohibition.

The excluded child

15–17-year-olds who can't get a token are the subject of Thesis 6; children whose parents are uninvolved should be added to the groups on the card. Claude Opus 5.5, Grok and studio agent 3 raised this group; the other voices took no stance.

And the ban itself?

Here too there is no single view. Gemini 3.8 Flash argued a blanket ban doesn't protect children, leaving them more vulnerable by pushing them behind adult IDs. Gemini 3.1 Pro and the Gemini app framed the regulation positively, as rebuilding the public sphere on the state's trust infrastructure. Claude Opus 5.5 combined the two: this is an infrastructure decision; both power and risk.

Where we ended upThe ban is defensible. But success is not measured by the ban itself. The ban's strongest defence is in the next section.

The counter-voice: the strongest defence of the ban

Most models met on "what protects is design, not the ban." To see how much of that agreement is real and how much comes from the shape of our question, we gave a separate model (Claude Opus 5.5) one task: write the strongest defence of the ban, with sources. The defence is built in four steps.

  1. The problem is not individual children but everyone being there. The cost to a child of not being on a platform grows with their friends being there. Experiments with university students on TikTok and Instagram showed that even active users may prefer the platform not to exist at all; the authors call this a "product-market trap."[27] Ban advocates call the same situation a "collective action trap": every exception allowing a younger age with parental consent traps everyone again.[28] This is a coordination problem, and a coordination problem is solved by a rule applied to everyone at once. A design rule leaves the child inside the network.
  2. Law changes norms, and norms move before behaviour. The sentence "not before 15" gives parents a non-negotiable footing. Even the 21 drinking age, which everyone knows is circumvented, clearly reduced consumption and the main harms in the US.[22] The tobacco-21 limit also markedly lowered regular smoking among 18–20-year-olds who had tried smoking; the effect was larger for those whose closest friends smoked at 16.[29] In Australia in the ban's third month, among 10–15-year-olds those saying "most of my peers use social media" fell from 85.1% to 74%, and those saying "someone without an account is missing out" from 43.3% to 36.3%. Among parents, those saying "it's normal for my child's peers to have accounts" fell from 73.4% to 60%. The report adds a warning: perception can change faster than behaviour, and in month three the accompanying behaviour change is still limited.[23] The age limit's effect is expected to show most in the 10–12 cohort that hasn't started yet.
  3. A one-question rule is the cheapest to enforce. Those who must implement design rules are the party whose revenue comes from the child's attention. As rules multiply, each article can be watered down separately. "Is this account under 15" is a single, clear question.
  4. The dependency runs the other way. Article 8 applies only if the user's age is known. So the design layer rests on age infrastructure: the ban works without design rules, design rules don't work without age information.

The counter-voice also wrote its own weak points. The collective-trap evidence comes from adult students, not children. The alcohol analogy is lame too: alcohol's point of sale is physical and enforceable, an account's is not. Most important, in Australia the share of parents of children who said they used social media in the last four weeks who don't know it rose from 23.3% to 33.3%.[4] The ban may be turning visible use into invisible use.

What this objection taught us

  • We split the question in two. "Ban or design?" set the two up as rivals. Yet one is the other's condition: design rules don't work unless age is known. The right question should have been not "which" but "which carries which."
  • We didn't weigh the evidence equally. We looked at the ban by its outcome: use after three months. We looked at design rules by their input: 128 changes on platforms. Applying the same measure to both would strain design rules too.[20][21]
  • No model openly opposed. We measured every model's stance on every thesis by quotation from its answer. In 48 pairings there are 33 agreements, 7 partial agreements and 8 non-addressings; no open opposition. In our first draft we had written some models as opposing. But that was the "counter-thesis" section our question asked of every answer: models had written objections to their own theses, not opposed the thesis. So we had the real objection written by a separate counter-voice.
  • Independence is less than we thought. Of eight answers, four came from the same model family (Claude) and three from another (Gemini). Asking similar models the same question doesn't yield as many independent views as there are voices. An unverified sentence had also entered our question; we removed it from the piece, but it may have affected the answers.
  • We changed one recommendation. Two perception questions and the cohort that has no account yet were added to the baseline measurement (second recommendation). Both views will be tested most there.

What we recommend

Five recommendations, each with its first-year measure. The common point: most rules are already written; what's missing is data showing whether they work.

  1. Make the token specification open and auditable

    The token should be single-use, short-lived, unlinkable across platforms and verifiable offline. Fix the form at two bits (15+, 18+). Publish the specification and sample code.

    First-year measureWas the guide in Article 12 published before 1 November? Did an independent audit answer "no" to "can two tokens on two platforms be linked to the same person"?

  2. Take the baseline before entry into force; measure exposure, not accounts

    An independent panel (a university or the Türkiye Statistical Institute (TÜİK)) should measure before 1 November and repeat at months 6 and 12: use at ages 10–14 and 15–17, night-time use, use of out-of-scope apps, the "my family doesn't know" share and sleep.

    Add two perception questions: "Do most of your peers use social media?" and "Does someone without an account miss out?" Track separately the 10–12 cohort that has no account yet; the age limit's effect shows there first. Without a baseline no claim of success or failure can be measured.

    First-year measureWas the baseline published? How much did use fall at 12–14? (Comparison: about 4–5 points in Australia.) Did the age of first account change?

  3. Make Article 8 testable from outside

    The ban on infinite feeds, the ban on personalisation, private accounts and the live-streaming restriction are already written. Now comes enforcement: keep a scorecard by platform and article with "mystery shopper" test accounts, published every three months.

    First-year measureArticle compliance rate on the five largest platforms. Complaints of unwanted contact from strangers to accounts of ages 15–17.

  4. Make false rejection and exclusion visible

    Open a second path outside e-Devlet (the school administration or PTT, the national postal service). Publish monthly the number of those who can't get a token and those who appeal; the annual statistics in Article 10 are not enough for this.

    First-year measureToken failure rate. Median time to resolve appeals.

  5. Measure handover and migration honestly; bring in the school

    Manage token sharing not by prohibition but by tracking its rate. Give parents and students a short, tested programme at school: how to open the parental tools in Article 9, what changes in the child's account.

    First-year measureIn the survey, the questions "whose token did you enter with" and "which app did you move to." Number of schools joining the programme.

What would change our mind?

A thesis's worth lies in saying up front what would refute it. For each of the six theses this measure is written on its own card: the highlighted boxes in the thesis cards. The same holds for views outside the cards; if the data below arrives, we withdraw or soften them.

  • Ifin Australia's month-12 data the perception "most of my peers use it" has fallen markedly and account opening has fallen in the newly starting 10–12 cohort,Thenwe underrated the age limit's effect.
  • Ifin Türkiye, with high compliance with Article 8 and no change in the under-15 account rate, harm indicators (unwanted contact from strangers, night-time use) fall,Thenit is design that protects, and the counter-voice's defence weakens.
  • Ifin the first year enforcement decisions are published with their reasons and the number of children affected,Thenthe "unauditable infrastructure" concern eases.
  • Ifthe baseline measurement has already been taken in public view,Thenour second recommendation isn't needed.

How this piece was made

The topic was handled with a Spark round: the same question was asked separately of AI models that couldn't see one another's answers, and 8 independent answers came back. Then a separate model was given the task of writing the strongest objection to the view the answers agreed on. The sources in all answers were checked one by one. Each model's stance on each thesis was also measured by quotation from its answer; the agreements and divergences were combined in this piece.
